<b>Date:</b> September 1969<br>
<b>Author:</b> Atsuro Sasaki



<b><u>Rotary SS Takes to the Tomei and Mountain Roads</b></u>

The Familia Rotary SS is the third model in Toyo Kogyo’s rotary series, following the Cosmo and the Rotary Coupe.

The smooth performance characteristic of the rotary engine has already been amply demonstrated by those first two cars.

Would the Familia Rotary SS live up to that established reputation and show the same ability? It was with considerable anticipation that I approached this road test...

We Japanese have a weakness for the "latest thing." <i>Haikara</i> may not mean much to younger readers, so let us put it another way: we are always eager for something new. And we are also a people rather prone, at times, to wanting things beyond our means.

That is why motorization has developed on such a remarkable scale in less than twenty years after the war, and why so many high-performance cars are sold in relation to the level of the national income. A novice may start out perfectly content as long as the engine runs and the car drives, but that attitude soon changes.

“After all, what I had until the other day was a five-year-old Familia 800. You can’t drive something like that on the expressway. That’s why I went and bought this one.”

And so, the car now finding itself in the spotlight is the Rotary SS.

<b><u>A Bright New Star in the Wide-Ranging Familia Series</b></u>

Toyo Kogyo acquired the manufacturing rights to the NSU Wankel rotary engine and began developing it some years ago. Its first rotary offering was the Cosmo Sport. It attracted worldwide attention—and that is no exaggeration. Even NSU itself had not yet produced such a fully realized rotary-engined car. Then, the following year—last year—the Mazda Familia Rotary Coupe appeared as what might be called a more popular version. I am told that more than 10,000 have already been sold. It seems there are certainly plenty of people who cannot resist something new. 

But that does not seem to be the whole explanation. With 2 rotors displacing 491cc each, it produces a formidable 100ps/7000rpm. Its quick, sporting response is another quality unique to the rotary. These attractions must surely have stirred the enthusiasm of the growing number of sporting-car enthusiasts throughout the country.

For its third offering, Toyo Kogyo has produced an even more accessible model. One gets the impression that, year by year, the company has been carefully gauging public response and gradually drawing back the curtain.

The name "SS" presumably stands for “Sports Sedan.” As the Familia name suggests, the car began life as a popular model aimed at the "my car" segment of private owners, and, as everyone knows, it has since developed into a remarkably wide-ranging series.

Perhaps following the precedent set by the Ford Mustang, there are now so many variations that it is difficult even to count them all, from the original model through to the four-door Deluxe. Both 1000 and 1200 engines are offered, and now Toyo Kogyo has added its prized rotary engine as well. There seems to be something for everyone, from those stretching beyond their means to those moving naturally up the automotive ladder.

Whereas the Familia Rotary Coupe, at 700,000 yen, was about as expensive as anything in its class, the SS comes in 62,000 yen cheaper, at 638,000 yen. At the same time, however, an E-type Coupe has been introduced at 660,000 yen, creating an intermediate step in the range.

For those who find 700,000 yen to be a little too much, there is the 660,000 model; for those with families or who need a car for business, there is the SS. The customer can choose freely.

“Our basic intention is to encourage wider use of the rotary engine. We want people to understand that it is not a car intended solely for drivers with special skill or for sporting enthusiasts.” So says a public-relations representative at the company’s Tokyo branch. Toyo Kogyo certainly seems determined to leave no stone unturned.

So what is the Familia Rotary SS actually like?

As a guide, I offer the following report on a varied, moderately long touring route: from Tokyo via the Tomei Expressway to the Atsugi-Odawara Road, through Hakone over the Otome Pass to Gotemba, then back to Tokyo on the Tomei.

<b><u>A Well-Executed Interior—But with Some Thoughtless Details</b></u>

It must have been a little after 10:30am when we left the Yaesu side of Tokyo Station. Driving through the city in the second wave of the morning rush, I had my hands full simply getting accustomed to the car.

Let us begin by comparing it with the Rotary Coupe I tested last year.

The Coupe had what was called a "T-shaped" dashboard layout, with a center console flowing into an instrument panel that extended horizontally across the cabin. In the SS, this has been replaced by a single horizontal instrument panel. Four circular gauges are arranged within an elongated hexagonal binnacle: from the right, water temperature, speedometer, tachometer and fuel level. To their left is the radio, with the heater controls below. All of these are recessed within a crash pad about 5cm deep. The panel between the instruments is black, as is the vinyl leather covering the crash pad. The material and surface finish of the latter are such that reflections in the windshield should present no problem.

The panel layout is quite conservative, but when seated in a proper driving position there is little difficulty in reading the instruments.

The steering wheel is wood, with a somewhat thick rim. Its spokes, however, are bare aluminum. This may lend a more sporting appearance, but it also seems rather at odds with the care taken to eliminate reflections from the instrument glass. I would have preferred the spokes to be finished in matte black.

Below the gauges is a panel painted in the body color—in the case of this car, white. It is simply left as pressed, with its lower edge folded inward, without any finishing. This is not an area one ordinarily touches, but when working on the wiring or connectors the edge seemed sharp enough to cut one’s hand. It is a minor detail, but it strikes me as showing a slight lack of consideration in a family car.

The contrast between this white body-colored panel and the black crash padding is also a little too pronounced. At the very least, would it not have been better to cover this area in the same material as the pad? It was overcast throughout the day, so it was not too bothersome this time, but I remember driving another car with a similar color scheme on the Tomei and Meishin and finding it extremely distracting and visually tiring.

Perhaps I am overly sensitive to such things because I often undertake long-distance tours. But when one considers the fatigue involved in sustained high-speed driving over long distances, I would like to see even the slightest reduction in the driver's visual—and ultimately psychological—burden. It is for that reason that I make this criticism.

<b><u>Smooth Acceleration—and a Quiet "SS"</b></u>

In other respects, I found very little to criticize.

Once past the intersection at Seta in Tamagawa and onto the Tomei Expressway, the rotary engine really began to reveal its strengths. Particularly after leaving the Tokyo toll gate, 7km from the entrance, its excellent response brought the car up to expressway speed almost immediately.

I normally drive a well-used 1300cc sedan, but it could never make a dash like this from the Tokyo gate on the Tomei. Nor could it match the smoothness with which the rotary gathers revs.

There were three people on board, along with cameras and other equipment, so the load was lighter than its full five-passenger capacity. Even so, the SS seemed to glide effortlessly away from the cars that had started alongside us.

The car I drove was still new, with less than 1,000km on it, but Toyo Kogyo assured me that “it is sufficiently run in to be driven properly now,” so I tried it under a variety of high-speed conditions.

Following a truck traveling at 80km/h, I accelerated to pass at 100km/h. At 80km/h the engine is turning at less than 3000rpm. As I pressed the accelerator lightly and swept easily past, a glance at the gauges showed 4200rpm and something like 120km/h. At the same speed, we took measurements with a tachymeter-equipped wristwatch and obtained a figure of about 115km/h. 

(Note: Along the edge of the expressway are small numbered markers placed at 100m intervals, with luminous markings down to 1m. By reading the figures at each kilometer and taking the corresponding speed from the scale around the stopwatch, road speed can be measured. On the Tomei, these kilometer posts in both directions are measured from Tokyo.)

“Effortless” is perhaps the word that best describes this acceleration. It may be that this is simply the difference between a reciprocating engine, in which the up-and-down motion of the pistons is converted into rotation of the crankshaft, and a rotary engine, in which a triangular rotor turns within its housing.

I tried cruising continuously at an indicated 110km/h. The engine note is quiet. There is no booming resonance inside the cabin. It is quiet enough to hear the sound of the air entering through the ventilators.

Speaking of the ventilators, their airflow may well be the strongest of any car in this class. Our car was fitted with air conditioning, but the ventilation was so effective that we left it switched off. Judging from the size of the outlets, I had not expected such a strong flow of air, and it came as a surprise.

On the expressway, many drivers close their windows because of the high level of engine and wind noise. Naturally, I doubt anyone wants to drive at 100km/h with the windows fully open, but in most cars the ventilation is so inadequate that unless a window is opened slightly, it is difficult to bring the desired amount of air to one’s face. This is a common source of dissatisfaction. With the Familia’s ventilation set to maximum and directed at the face, however, it remained comfortable even in the muggy, overcast weather.

<b><u>Engine Braking: Little Different from a Reciprocating-Engined Car</b></u>

From the Atsugi Interchange, we took the Atsugi-Odawara Road, then climbed in one stretch via the Tokyu Hakone Turnpike to Taikanzan.

Although the Atsugi-Odawara Road is a toll road, there are places along the way with at-grade intersections. Imagining that a car were suddenly to cross just in front of us at one of these, I tried rapid deceleration and tested the engine-braking effect.

Downshifts are smooth. 

For a new car with less than 1,000km on it, the gearshift is remarkably easy to operate. Not once during the entire journey of some 300km did I find a gear reluctant to engage.

The gear-lever knob is perfectly spherical. It fits neatly into the palm, though some people may prefer a more elongated shape. The recent trend seems to be moving away from spherical knobs. There is also a rubber insert in the hole in the knob, which grips the end of the lever. This has the advantage of “preventing transmission vibration from reaching the palm,” but in a car like this, where frequent gear changes are made, there is also the drawback that over time the rubber can deteriorate, making the knob easy to pull off. For this reason, owners of earlier Bluebird SS and SSS models used to remove the original knob and screw on a wooden one instead. (This type is no longer used on Nissan floor-shift models.)

Now onto engine braking.

In a reciprocating engine, the up-and-down motion of the pistons works through the connecting rods and crankshaft, whereas in a rotary it is simply the rotor itself that turns. There is less resistance. This naturally leads one to wonder:

“Doesn’t that mean the engine braking will be weak?”

But that concern proved unfounded. Strictly speaking, it does feel somewhat weaker than with a reciprocating engine, but there is definite engine-braking effect. This was, after all, only a road impression report, and we were not driving with another reciprocating-engined car to make direct comparisons. As a result, I cannot say exactly how much difference there is. Even so, whether traveling at 100km/h on the Tomei, descending from Taikanzan on the Turnpike toward Moto-Hakone, climbing and descending through the Hakone mountains, or negotiating the succession of sweeping bends on the descent from Sengokuhara via the Otome Road to Gotemba, I felt that the engine braking effect was perfectly usable.

Last year, when I drove a lap of the Shuto Expressway while testing the Rotary Coupe, both I and Mr. N of the Metropolitan Expressway Public Corporation, who accompanied me, were quite interested in the question of engine braking. But on the largely level Shuto Expressway, we could not reach any definite conclusions.

“Do you think it feels different from an ordinary car?”

“Not particularly.”

That was about as far as the discussion went. This time, however, we tried it over a variety of road surfaces, bends and descents. The conclusion of the three of us aboard was that it may feel just slightly weaker, but in practical use the difference is hardly noticeable. We can reasonably conclude that there is entirely adequate braking resistance.

<b><u>Bringing the Rotary Within Easier Reach</b></u>

On the mountain roads of Hakone, each of the three of us examined the car in our own way, both as a sporting sedan and, setting aside its rotary engine, as a family sedan.

We drove over some notably rough roads, explored its cornering characteristics through the curves, and put it through different patterns of shifting. Although each of us naturally had his own impressions and expressed them differently, we all agreed that the car makes for an enjoyable "SS" and is equally satisfying as a powerful family car. The front seats are quite good for a car of this class and price, and rear-seat space is not unduly tight. With a cabin width of 1265mm, however, three adults across the rear will feel cramped. There is no denying that.

The rear suspension felt rather soft. If one were to ask for more, I think a slightly firmer setting would be preferable for the rougher treatment a sporting sedan is likely to receive.

From Gotemba we rejoined the Tomei Expressway. It was the two-hour afternoon rush period, with traffic running at around 13-15 vehicles per kilometer. Everyone was traveling at either the speed limit or somewhat above it, so from the Ashigara Service Area, we drove straight through to Tokyo in one stretch. This is not a particularly long distance, but the ease with which the car maintains 80-100km/h on only a light throttle opening must be counted as one of its virtues.

In terms of body size, this is a 1.0-1.4-liter-class car. Ordinarily, in this category, sustaining continuous running at 100km/h soon brings fatigue through the combined effects of noise, vibration, maintaining speed, and the other demands of high-speed driving. In the Rotary SS, that fatigue is reduced by a considerable margin. It is something to be grateful for.

After only 300km and half a day with the car, I may seem to be praising it a little too highly.

Even so, Toyo Kogyo deserves credit for bringing a rotary-engined car into the popular-car class at this price.

As I wrote at the outset, Japanese car owners are eager for the latest thing and have expensive tastes. It would be fair to say that another highly attractive product has now been added for just such people.

The body is that of the Familia Sedan, with a rotary heart. But the grille and tail lamps retain the styling of the Rotary Coupe. For those who would like to buy the Coupe but have hesitated because of their budget or practical considerations, the rotary engine has suddenly become much more accessible. It is also a car into which current owners of the Familia 1000 or 1200 could move up without any great difficulty. In that sense, it is perhaps best described as a car in which speed can be enjoyed with ease—provided, that is, that one exercises just a little care in handling its rather excessive power...
