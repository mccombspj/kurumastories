<b>Publication:</b> Motor Fan<br>
<b>Format:</b> Touring Report<br>
<b>Date:</b> January 1969<br>
<b>Author:</b> Shuzo Araki, Jun Todoroki









Isuzu's top-class grand tourer, the 117 Coupe, has finally gone on sale. Its graceful lines, styled by the renowned Italian <i>carrozzeria</i> Ghia, have inspired hopes for production ever since the car appeared at the 13th Tokyo Motor Show. Taking this opportunity to explore the 117 Coupe's true capabilities, Motor Fan undertook a demanding 570km touring test covering a wide variety of roads and conditions, including expressways, mountain roads, and rough surfaces.

<b><u>Into a Rainy Night</b></u>

The brand-new Isuzu 117 Coupe, finished in a pale cream color, set off from rain-soaked Tokyo on November 9, in the gathering darkness of a late-autumn evening.

We were setting out in search of roads that would allow us to put this top-class grand tourer fully to the test.

The odometer already showed more than 1,300km. We were grateful that the manufacturer had thoughtfully completed the running-in period for us.

Our route first took us along the Tomei Expressway toward Atsugi. The rain grew steadily heavier. From there, we followed National Highway 246 to Matsuda, then Route 255 to Odawara...

To bring out the car's high performance, we naturally chose the Hakone Turnpike, well known for its steep gradients, for the climb into the mountains.

Most of our previous test drives had been limited to daylight hours. But with a touring car, it is impossible to make a proper assessment without testing the comfort of traveling in it, as the name itself implies. Night driving is therefore an important consideration, as is driving in the rain.

For this test, we deliberately chose to make the journey from Tokyo to Hakone at night. And although it may seem strange to call it fortunate, the rain provided exactly the conditions we wanted.

As we made our way out of Tokyo, the smooth, Italian-styled lines of the Coupe stood out vividly under the streetlights, clearly attracting attention from passersby.

<b><u>The Essence of Fastback Styling</b></u>

<b>Araki:</b> This car was first unveiled two years ago at the 13th Tokyo Motor Show, but it's remarkable how it still doesn't look the least bit dated.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Quite so. Recently we've seen a number of new fastback cars, such as the Sunny Coupe and Corolla Sprinter, but this one has a freshness about it that's entirely different. Of course, it's hardly fair to compare them in the first place...

<b>Araki:</b> The lines from the front through to the sides are very natural. The word "graceful" seems particularly appropriate.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Although I do think it has an almost overly delicate look. It's a refined kind of beauty that will appeal to almost everyone, rather than making any particularly striking impression.

<b>Araki:</b> Even so, the way the roofline flows down and blends into the rear fenders could be called the very essence of fastback styling.

<b>Todoroki:</b> The use of stainless steel to give the rear pillars a slender appearance is particularly effective. In fact, the same can be said of the entire design. It's remarkably clean and uncluttered.

<b>Araki:</b> The front grille has a fine chrome strip running horizontally across a black mesh background, with the traditional lion emblem in the center. It's a very tasteful, rather clever touch. But viewed directly from behind, despite the overall design being so clean and elegant, the pronounced "coke-bottle" treatment at the rear makes it look a little heavy.

<b>Todoroki:</b> No, I wouldn't agree with that. I think it's more a natural flow of the lines than a deliberate coke-bottle shape, and the flared fenders don't upset the balance. From the front, the car looks extremely slender, while from behind it's quite voluptuous. To me, the skill lies in how well those two impressions have been brought together.

<b>Araki:</b> The ground clearance is 180mm, comparable to that of a sedan, yet the overall height is very low at just 1320mm. With the current layout, headroom is rather limited, but surely the seats could be lowered further. Of course, there's a compromise involved, since that would visually lengthen the nose from the driver's perspective and make the car more difficult to drive.

<b><u>High-Grade Appointments</b></u>

Heavy rain lashed the Tomei Expressway. The section between Tokyo and Atsugi is a wide highway, with three lanes in each direction, but there is no road lighting. The powerful beams of the four headlights pierced the darkness ahead.

The cockpit design presents the driver with a wraparound dashboard, a first for a Japanese production car. Directly ahead are two large round gauges: on the right, a speedometer calibrated to 220km/h, and on the left, a tachometer reading to 8000rpm, with the yellow zone beginning at 6500rpm and the red zone at 7000rpm.

The panel to the left is angled toward the driver and contains four smaller gauges. Starting nearest the tachometer, these are the coolant temperature gauge, oil pressure gauge, fuel gauge and ammeter. Below them are pull switches for the windshield wipers and lights. To the right of the speedometer is another inward-facing panel, housing the clock.

The instrument panel is finished in genuine Taiwanese camphor wood, with a beautiful cloud-like grain. Walnut is used for the steering wheel and gearshift knob, while the stainless-steel horn bar, with its open-spoke design, provides a distinctive accent. The result is a superb combination of luxurious appointments and sporting character.

Lowering a car's height to enhance the beauty of its styling inevitably reduces the space available for its occupants. Compensating for this requires careful attention to details such as reclining the occupants' upper bodies and lowering their hip points.

In this car, headroom has been set at 850mm, a figure worth noting because it is the FIA's dimensional standard for distinguishing a 2+2 from a genuine four-seater. With this 850mm clearance, and nothing more beyond the "give" in the seat cushions, the design can be said to provide adequate accommodation not only in the front seats, but in the rear as well.

The center console houses an AM/FM radio and the heater controls, with a short gearshift lever jutting from its center. Everywhere one looks, the interior seems to be filled with expensive-looking fittings.

<b><u>Seats That Fit the Body</b></u>

<b>Todoroki:</b> First of all, the seats are excellent! I don't think it would be an exaggeration to say that they're more comfortable than those of any Japanese car introduced to date.

<b>Araki:</b> I agree. The way the padding bulges slightly around the lower back and fits snugly against the body is particularly pleasing. The only problem is that the reclining adjustment has too few positions. At my height of 170cm, I found that the backrest was always just a little too far reclined or too upright, and I couldn't quite find the ideal position.

<b>Todoroki:</b> On a car as expensive as this, I'd like to see a continuously adjustable reclining mechanism. A friction-type arrangement would solve the problem easily.

I also like the gearshift lever. It's positioned exactly where the left hand naturally falls from the steering wheel, and there's really nothing about it that can be criticized.

<b>Araki:</b> I do have a slight objection to the shift pattern. It's different from the usual arrangement, and when shifting down from third to second, you sometimes feel the lever catch against the reverse gate.

<b>Todoroki:</b> The clutch is neither too heavy nor too light. It's mechanically operated, but it has a very pleasant feel.

<b>Araki:</b> A great deal of attention has clearly been paid to the pedal layout as a whole. If I could ask for one more thing, it would be a footrest for the left foot.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Given the character of this car, heel-and-toe operation may not be necessary, but the pedals are arranged so that it's very easy to do if you want to.

<b>Araki:</b> Here's something you wouldn't notice unless you drove at night. On the Florian, Isuzu fitted illuminated rings to make the switches easier to locate. That was a good idea, so I wonder why they didn't adopt it on the 117 Coupe.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Well, once you're used to the controls, it isn't a problem. After all, most other cars are the same in that respect. Incidentally, the windshield wipers are very good.

<b>Araki:</b> They sweep a wide area, and they park neatly out of the way. Also, they clear the glass right up close to the right-hand pillar, so you can really see the right-hand fender mirror.

<b>Todoroki:</b> And operating the washer automatically activates the wipers. I did think that the windshield pillars were rather thick, though. Presumably that's a matter of structural strength, but they create fairly large blind spots.

<b>Araki:</b> That slightly "overengineered" quality is typical of Isuzu. But it's not just the pillars. Even the dashboard, despite using expensive materials, still has a few crude details in its finish. The warning lamps in particular looked rather cheap. At the very least, I'd like to see them given a diamond-cut finish.

<b><u>A Steering Wheel with an Excellent Feel</b></u>

<b>Todoroki:</b> It's certainly a high-grade panel, made of Taiwanese camphor wood backed with aluminum. But I'd like to see a little more care taken with the finishing, such as the edges and the screw heads. Still, I think the warning lamps for the doors (pale orange), parking brake (red) and defogger (light blue) have a pleasing, soft appearance. And the defogger was quite effective, too.

<b>Araki:</b> Speaking of expensive materials, the walnut steering wheel also makes a very good impression.

<b>Todoroki:</b> At 39cm in diameter, it's on the small side, and the rim is just the right thickness. It really has an excellent feel, making the most of the quality of the material.

<b>Araki:</b> What did you think of the rear seat?

<b>Todoroki:</b> The seats are semi-bucket shaped, rather like those in a Porsche, and they're reasonably comfortable. But at my height of 173cm, the lack of headroom was certainly noticeable.

<b>Araki:</b> I'll grant that the rear seats have been designed as more than an afterthought. But I imagine they'll get hot in summer, with that much glass at such a steep angle.

<b>Todoroki:</b> I'd like to see the same kind of sun-reflecting coating used on the Corona Mark II 1900 Hardtop SL applied on this car. The recessed armrests alongside the rear seat are very well designed. They're convenient for storing small items, too.

<b>Araki:</b> The rear seatbacks also recline, but the adjustment changes their angle only very slightly, so it doesn't help much. I also found the mechanism difficult to operate on my own.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Even so, the fact that the seatbacks can be folded completely forward to form a broad, flat luggage area connected to the trunk is an essential feature of a grand touring car. It's especially useful in rainy weather, as we experienced on this trip.

<b>Araki:</b> I thought it was a luxurious touch that even the trunk was fully carpeted.

<b><u>Successfully Made Easy to Handle</b></u>

We spent the night in Hakone. The following day dawned clear and bright, with ideal weather for driving. We believe that the best service we can offer readers in a road test is to cover as much distance as possible, over as many different kinds of roads as possible. With that in mind, we started the engine at 5:00am.

The temperature was 2°C, and the car had been parked outdoors all night, but starting was remarkably easy. Following the owner's manual, we pulled out the choke and turned the ignition key without touching the accelerator. The engine started immediately, and despite the cold, the revs rose smoothly without requiring a lengthy warm-up. Cars like this are often difficult to start and prone to flooding, but in this respect the 117 Coupe is exceptionally easy to handle.

The carburetors are twin-choke Mikuni-Solex 40PHH units, with 40mm throttle bores, and performance is more than sufficient for thoroughly enjoyable sporting driving.

Among the advantages of the Solex are its resistance to changes in the attitude of the car and its high volumetric efficiency. On the other hand, its poor starting characteristics are well known, particularly in racing.

But in a car intended for normal drivers, like the Isuzu 117 Coupe, the starting problem must be overcome if the car is to be considered practical. To compensate, a starting device is fitted, in which the throttle valve serves the function performed by the choke in a conventional carburetor. As a result, it is essential not to pump the accelerator. As long as the driver understands this, there should be no difficulty in starting the engine.

We crossed Otome Pass to Gotemba, crossed National Highway 246, and climbed toward Kagosaka Pass by way of Subashiri.

It was on these gradients that the exceptional character of the 1600cc DOHC engine became clear.

With two people on board, the car weighs a substantial 1050kg, perhaps rather too much for a four-seater coupe. Yet from around 70km/h, a gentle press on the accelerator is enough to send the engine revving effortlessly, whether on a steep climb or through a tight corner. It gathers speed with the smoothness of an electric motor, without the slightest sign of strain.

<b><u>Isuzu's Tradition Lives On in the DOHC Engine</b></u>

What is the secret? The engine uses the block from the Bellett 1600GT, with a DOHC valve mechanism to withstand higher engine speeds. But the underlying Isuzu philosophy—an emphasis on strong low-speed torque—has not been compromised in the slightest.

<b>Araki:</b> They've essentially given the Bellett GT engine a "super tune," but it must have taken a great deal of effort to achieve this much using a block originally designed for a pushrod engine.

<b>Todoroki:</b> With a production target of only 50 cars a month, making use of existing components must be a necessity to keep costs down. In a sense, they've taken the sort of approach a tuning shop might use and, as a manufacturer, taken it as far as a conversion to DOHC. This allows the engine to develop more power through higher revs, while retaining the strong low-speed pulling power that has always been an Isuzu tradition.

<b>Araki:</b> The same can be said of the transmission. The relatively low gearing makes city driving easy, while the engine's ability to rev takes care of high-speed performance. So they've succeeded in making it a car that anyone can drive, yet one that can also stand up to hard driving.

Still, I can't help feeling that a fifth gear would be desirable. It would make things considerably quieter at high speeds. After all, this is a car with relatively low gearing, and the engine runs at fairly high revs.

<b>Todoroki:</b> On the other hand, the engine is so flexible that a 5-speed transmission may not be strictly necessary. But from the standpoint of fuel economy, noise, and the class of car this is, it would certainly be preferable.

There's no question that the engine has a very smooth, gentle feel, and you can always drive with plenty in reserve on half throttle. If you want to, you can push it as hard as you like, but somehow the car puts you in a mood where you no longer feel inclined to hurry.

<b>Araki:</b> That's true. Rather than sharpness, I think the real attraction of this engine is its tremendous power held in reserve. But noise is a problem, although this may be due to the transmission. I was particularly conscious of the booming noise during engine braking.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Still, the sound itself is quite pleasant, so I didn't especially mind it.

<b>Araki:</b> For a top-class grand touring car, quietness is an important requirement. It was a little disappointing to find that it gave much the same impression as the Bellett in this respect...

<b>Todoroki:</b> Even so, I think it's worth noting that the low-speed tuning is very good for an engine fitted with Solex carburetors, and there's no noticeable induction noise, either.

<b><u>Climbing the Fuji Subaru Line</b></u>

We followed National Highway 139 to Fujiyoshida, then made the climb up the Fuji Subaru Line to the fifth station of Mount Fuji. Stretching for more than 29km, this fully paved, two-lane highland highway is a first-class tourist road in terms of both its scenery and its scale.

As we climbed, the surrounding landscape changed from forests of red pine to stands of white birch, and eventually to open country covered with low shrubs. Higher still, the rugged mountainside appeared, strewn with volcanic rock.

The fifth station stands at an altitude of 2,340m, and there was already snow on the ground. Throughout the climb, we were able to enjoy the thoroughbred performance of the 117 Coupe to the full. Even at 90km/h in second gear and 120km/h in third, there was still plenty of accelerator travel in reserve. We found ourselves effortlessly reeling in other cars slowly making their way up the mountain.

The acceleration was so satisfying that it constantly tempted us to push harder. Body roll during cornering was only slight. The rear suspension is a rigid axle with asymmetrical semi-elliptic leaf springs, but thanks to the low center of gravity, the excellent matching of the tires, and the appropriate choice of spring rates, the road holding is so good that there is virtually nothing to criticize.

To prevent axle wind-up when starting off, torque rods are fitted between the side members and the rear axle housing. Forces are absorbed through the deflection of rubber bushes at either end of the rods.

Naturally, the use of these rods also serves to increase the lateral rigidity of the leaf-spring suspension.

<b><u>Excellent Road Holding Is Another Distinctive Feature</b></u>

From the fifth station, we continued along the Takizawa Forest Road. Even on rough surfaces, the road holding remained good, and the generous ground clearance allowed us to drive with relatively little concern. However, the road surface was frozen, and since we had brought no tire chains, we were forced to turn back before venturing too far.

<b>Araki:</b> The rear wheels certainly tend to hop on rough roads. Isuzu has plenty of experience with independent suspension, so I wonder why they didn't adopt it for this car.

<b>Todoroki:</b> That's true, but I think this suspension has a quality of its own that makes it at least as good as a well-designed independent system. After all, given the nature of this car, it's hardly likely to spend much time on rough roads. What particularly impressed me was the tires. I think they deserve much of the credit for the excellent road holding. Even in some very tight corners, they hardly squealed at all.

<b>Araki:</b> The 6.45H-14 tires they've used are fairly wide for a 1.6-liter car, which helps provide good grip. And the car doesn't seem to be affected much by irregularities in the road surface. That may be due to the gas-filled dampers, though.

<b>Todoroki:</b> The steering characteristics were close to neutral, with a mild tendency toward understeer, wouldn't you say?

<b>Araki:</b> I think it maintains its understeering tendency even under quite high lateral g. I did find the steering a little heavy for a recirculating-ball system.

<b>Todoroki:</b> I imagine they chose this setup to retain something of the sporting feel of rack-and-pinion steering while eliminating kickback from the road surface. In any case, I don't think the feel is at all bad.

<b>Araki:</b> One thing I noticed when we turned around on the forest road was that the turning circle is rather large.

<b>Todoroki:</b> At 5.2m, the turning radius certainly isn't small, but in practice I don't think it's much of a problem.

<b><u>A Tourer Suited to Japanese Conditions</b></u>

Descending the Subaru Line, we took National Highway 139 through the Fuji Five Lakes region. The mountains were ablaze with autumn colors, and the smooth, winding road threaded its way through the valleys. Then, suddenly, the curves came to an end, and the broad foothills of Mount Fuji opened out before us. With its summit crowned in white snow, Fuji looked just like a painting.

The Fujinomiya Toll Road stretched straight ahead through the pastures of the Asagiri Plateau. Passing Shiraito Falls along the way, we continued without stopping to the Tomei Expressway interchange at Fujinomiya.

Even in the occasional traffic around Fujinomiya, the 117 Coupe was as smooth and easy to drive as a normal sedan. This must surely be the result of Isuzu's efforts to make the car practical under Japan's particular conditions, with our often inconsistent and ill-matched roads.

Take, for example, the feel of the brakes. They inspire confidence at high speeds, yet even in crowded city streets they require little pedal effort and provide exceptionally good response. One cannot help thinking, "They've refined this to a remarkable degree."

Many foreign high-speed tourers, especially those from Europe, exhibit the somewhat uncertain braking feel characteristic of disc brakes at low speeds. In this respect, Isuzu's efforts to ensure that even an inexperienced driver would feel entirely at ease driving the 117 Coupe appear to have paid off.

The Fujinomiya–Shizuoka section of the Tomei Expressway is the only seaside highway currently open in Japan. Winds blowing in from Suruga Bay buffet cars mercilessly, and however good the road may be, failing to account for these crosswinds can sometimes lead to trouble.

Although the weather was perfectly clear that day, winds were blowing at more than 10m/s, and the sudden gusts were especially noticeable when emerging from tunnels.

But the 117 Coupe was different. Here, one could really appreciate the benefits of its aerodynamic shape. The faster we drove, the more the car seemed to cling to the road, with a reassuring sense of stability. Even after only a short distance, it was clear that crosswinds had remarkably little effect.

There was another surprise. At around 100km/h, we tried gradually opening the side windows, and were astonished to find that hardly any wind entered the car from the sides.

<b><u>Surprisingly Good Fuel Economy</b></u>

From there, we returned to Fujinomiya and made our way back to Tokyo via National Highway 1 and Hakone Pass. The total distance covered was 572.6km, and over the 367.6km section from Lake Yamanaka to Tokyo, we recorded fuel consumption of 9.054km/l. The manufacturer quotes an average touring fuel economy of 10km/l, and considering how hard we drove the car this time, it seems reasonable to expect that figure to be comfortably maintained under normal conditions. For a GT car like this, the 117 Coupe can be regarded as relatively economical on gasoline.

<b>Todoroki:</b> Once we were on the Tomei Expressway, I tried pressing the accelerator down fully. There wasn't any particularly fierce sensation of acceleration, but when I looked at the speedometer, I was surprised to see how fast we were actually going.

<b>Araki:</b> It has a very smooth, gentle character. Those who want sharpness above all else may find it unsatisfying, but isn't this just the sort of quality that gives European cars their unique appeal?

<b><u>Outstanding Stability</b></u>

<b>Todoroki:</b> Another distinctive feature is the lack of sensation of speed. Despite the large glass area, the driving position gives you a distant view of the road ahead. Combined with the exceptional stability, I think that's why the car doesn't feel as fast as it really is.

<b>Araki:</b> It was affected remarkably little by crosswinds. Do you suppose that's because of the body styling?

<b>Todoroki:</b> I think so. I think it's further proof of the excellence of Ghia's design. Safety is becoming an important issue, not only with this car, but I've always felt that the real priority should be safety that helps prevent an accident, rather than only protecting the occupants after an accident occurs. The 117 Coupe's resistance to crosswinds is a perfect example of that. Come to think of it, when that truck suddenly pulled into the passing lane, we had to brake very hard from high speed...

<b>Araki:</b> Yes, I had to stomp on the brakes without a moment's hesitation. The best way I can describe it is that it felt like a giant was pulling the car back. With braking like that, you can feel quite confident slowing down from high speeds.

<b>Todoroki:</b> The car is fitted with servo-assisted front disc brakes, and their dependability seems exceptionally high. The feel is excellent, too...

<b>Araki:</b> There was a little vibration through the body as we approached maximum speed.

<b>Todoroki:</b> I imagine that was peculiar to this particular car, and it didn't bother me very much.

One thing I forgot to mention was the wonderful sound of the horn. It has a dry, crisp note, quite unlike anything I've heard before. Once you've heard it, you'd immediately recognize it as belonging to a 117 Coupe. They've clearly given it a great deal of thought.

<b>Araki:</b> It's not only a pleasant sound, but one that carries very well, making it particularly suited to expressway driving. In any case, there are two or three minor details I'm not entirely happy with, but after driving more than 500km, I've come to appreciate just how good this car really is.

<b>Todoroki:</b> We've driven it under all sorts of conditions on this test, and in terms of its overall performance, I think it represents just what a grand touring car ought to be. And, well, at 1.72 million yen, it's quite a price, too...
